# Clarity checklist after Joseph M. Williams, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*

A one-page summary of the book's principles in our own words, for auditing Aravind's eBooks and blog posts. It is
combined with his voice profiles — spoken (notes/2026-10-28-voice-profile.md, from 7 YouTube videos' captions) and written (vp job, docs/voice-profile.md on task/voice-profile) — into the style guide the audits use. Where a principle and the
voice disagree, the voice wins unless the sentence is unclear.

## Sentences
1. **Characters as subjects.** The subject of a sentence names who or what acts: R, the function, you, the store
   manager. Not "The creation of a vector is done with `c()`" but "`c()` creates a vector."
2. **Actions as verbs.** Turn nouns made from verbs back into verbs: "perform an analysis of" → "analyze";
   "make a comparison" → "compare"; "the calculation of the mean" → "calculating the mean" or "R calculates the mean".
3. **Subject and verb early and close.** Get to the main subject and verb quickly; don't open with long clauses or
   put a long phrase between subject and verb.
4. **Old before new.** Start a sentence with something the reader already knows (from the previous sentence or the
   page) and end with the new information.
5. **Stress at the end.** Put the word or idea that matters most at the end of the sentence, where readers expect it.
   Don't end on a weak phrase ("…in this case", "…as well").

## Paragraphs and sections
6. **Steady topics.** The subjects of the sentences in a paragraph form a thread the reader can follow; avoid jumping
   between unrelated subjects.
7. **Point first.** A paragraph or section opens with a short issue and states its point at the end of that opening,
   so the reader knows what the rest supports. In tutorials: the problem, then the idea, then the code.
8. **Name the new term once, then keep it.** Introduce a technical term, define it in plain words, and use the same
   word every time after (no synonyms for variety).

## Concision
9. **Cut redundancy:** pairs that mean one thing ("each and every"), words implied by others ("future plans"),
   repeated ideas.
10. **Cut hedges and intensifiers** that add nothing ("basically", "really", "quite", "very", "it seems that",
    "it is important to note that").
11. **Cut talk about the text** ("In this section we will discuss…", "As mentioned above…") unless it orients a
    reader who needs it — at most one short line at the start of a chapter.
12. **Positive over negative.** "Keep the rows where…" not "Don't keep the rows that aren't…".

## Shape and grace
13. **Long sentences need shape.** Split a sentence that strings several clauses together; or use a list when the
    items are parallel. Items in a list or series have the same grammatical form.
14. **Vary length on purpose.** A short sentence after longer ones lands a point. Use it for the key idea.
15. **Concrete over abstract.** An example with real data or code beats an abstract statement of the same idea.

## For eBooks as the course supplement (Aravind, 2026-10-30)
16. **Deeper, not repeated.** A chapter adds what a course lesson leaves out (more cases, edge cases, why it works,
    alternatives), and doesn't just repeat the lesson.
17. **Findable.** A student arriving from a course lesson finds the concept quickly: clear headings named for what
    they want to do, one concept per section.
18. **Same words as the courses.** Use the courses' terms (C92 category/number column, C91 spread, C89 mistake/bug)
    and US spelling (C115), so a student moving between them isn't confused.
